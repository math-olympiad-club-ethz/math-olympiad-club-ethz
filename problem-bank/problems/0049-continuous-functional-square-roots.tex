\begin{problem}
    For each function \( g\in\left\{-id_{\mathbb{R}}, \exp, x\mapsto x^2-2\right\} \), determine
    whether there exists a continuous function \( f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} \) such that
    \( f\circ f = g \).
    \\\\
    \textbf{Bonus:} Solve the same problem with \( g\in\left\{\cos,\sin\right\} \).
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item[(a)] No, first \( f \) must be bijective. Prove that \( f\left(0\right) = 0 \) and
            investigate the sign of \( f\left(x\right) \) for \( x > 0 \).
        \item[(b)] Prove that \( f \) is strictly increasing and that \( \inf f = a \in
            \left(-\infty, 0\right) \), \( f\left(a\right) = 0 \). Fix an arbitrary strictly
            increasing function \( f_0 \in C\left(\left[a,0\right]\right) \) satisfying the
            conditions \( f_0\left(a\right) = 0 \) and \( f_0\left(0\right) = e^a \), and extend it
            to \( \mathbb{R} \) by using the equation.
        \item[(c)] Prove that \( f \) must be strictly increasing on \( [0, +\infty) \) and strictly
            decreasing on \( (-\infty, 0] \) and that \( f\left(0\right) \geq 0 \), which is
            impossible, because \( F \), and hence \( f \), takes negative values.
    \end{itemize}
    \textit{Bonus:} If \( F\left(x\right)=\cos\left(x\right) \) ? \subsection*{Solution:} To begin
    we do some preliminary work (some of the result are well known but for the sake of completeness
    we give the "proofs"):
    \\\\
    -The function \( \cos \) has a unique fixed point over \( \mathbb{R} \)
    (\( \exists!\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\,\left(\cos\left(\alpha\right)=\alpha\right) \)) which is
    moreover located between \( ]0,1[ \). We define in a classical manner (for this kind of fixed
    point problem) the function:
    \[\begin{tikzcd}
            {\varphi:} & {\mathbb{R}} & {\mathbb{R}} \\
            & x & {x-\cos(x)=x-\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(-1)^{i}\cdot\frac{x^{2\cdot i}}{(2\cdot i)!}}
            \arrow[from=1-2, to=1-3]
            \arrow[maps to, from=2-2, to=2-3]
        \end{tikzcd}\]
    \( \varphi \) is clearly \( C^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}\right) \) and even analytic. We study
    \( \varphi \) and show that in fact it has only one zero:
    \( |\varphi^{-1}\left[\left\{0\right\}\right]|=1 \) which will conclude the existence and
    uniqueness of the fixed point over \( \mathbb{R} \). Notice that
    \( \frac{d}{dx}\left(\varphi\right)=1-\sin\geq \underline{0} \). The theorem relating the type
    of monotonicity and the sign of the derivative tells us therefore that \( \varphi \) is
    increasing. As \( \varphi\left(0\right)=-1 \) and
    \( \varphi\left(1\right)=1-\cos\left(1\right)\overset{1\in]0;\frac{\pi}{2}[}{>}0 \), we have
    that:
    \[
        \varphi\big[]-\infty;0]\big]\subset]-\infty;-1]\subset]-\infty;0[
    \]
    and
    \[
        \varphi\big[[1;+\infty[\big]\subset[\varphi\left(1\right);+\infty\left[\subset\right]0;+\infty[
    \]
    Thus \( \varphi^{-1}\left[\left\{0\right\}\right]\subset]0;1[ \) and moreover we have by the
    intermediate value theorem (or the more general topological version: image of a connected set
    through a continuous function is connected and knowing that only the intervals are precisely the
    connected set of \( \left(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{R}}\right) \)) that
    \( 0\in\left[-1;\varphi\left(1\right)\right]\subset\varphi\big[\left[0;1\right]\big] \). This
    shows that \( |\varphi^{-1}\left[\left\{0\right\}\right]|\geq 1 \). We know by the property of
    \( \sin \):
    \[
        \forall
        x\in\mathbb{R}\big(\left(\frac{d}{dx}\left(\varphi\right)\right)\left(x\right)=0\leftrightarrow
        x\in\frac{\pi}{2}+2\cdot\pi\mathbb{Z}\big)
    \]
    Therefore we have the refinement that \( \varphi \) is strictly increasing over each connected
    component of \( \mathbb{R}\setminus\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+2\cdot\pi\mathbb{Z}\right) \). However
    \( ]\frac{\pi}{2}-2\cdot\pi;\frac{\pi}{2}[ \) is one of the connected component. This means that
    \( \varphi \) is strictly increasing over
    \( ]0;1\left[\subset\right]\frac{\pi}{2}-2\cdot\pi;\frac{\pi}{2}[ \) in particular it is
    injective. We saw that \( \varphi^{-1}\left[\left\{0\right\}\right]\subset]0;1[ \) so this means
    that \( |\varphi^{-1}\left[\left\{0\right\}\right]|=1 \). This concludes that there is a unique
    fixed point of \( \cos \) over \( \mathbb{R} \). For the culture, this fixed point is called the
    \( \textit{Dottie} \) number. The decimal expansion of the Dottie number is
    \( 0.739085133215160641655312087673873404... \) and one can show using some advanced techniques
    like the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem that it is also a transcendental number. How can we
    attain such a number ? It is easy: \( \cos \) is a contraction on \( \left[0;1\right] \) !
    Indeed its derivative (\( -\sin \)) is continuous therefore bounded over the compact
    \( \left[0;1\right] \) and it appears that those bound are strictly less than \( 1 \) ! To be
    more precise, let \( a,b\in\left[0;1\right] \), when \( a\neq b \) we have by the mean value
    theorem (\( \cos\in C^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}\right) \)) that \( \exists c\in]a;b[ \) such that
    \( -\sin\left(c\right)=\left(\frac{d}{dx}\cos\right)\left(c\right)=\frac{\cos\left(a\right)-\cos\left(b\right)}{a-b} \).
    Therefore we obtain:
    \[
        |\cos\left(a\right)-\cos\left(b\right)|\leq \max|-\sin|\big[\left[0;1\right]\big]\cdot|a-b|
    \]
    \[
        \overset{\sin\text{ is strictly increasing over } \left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right]}{=}\sin\left(1\right)\cdot|a-b|
    \]
    This bounds (which works even for \( a=b \)) shows that \( \cos \) is a contraction over
    \( \left[0;1\right] \) \textbf{provided} \( \sin\left(1\right)<1 \) which is the case since
    \( \sin \) is strictly increasing over \( \left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right] \) (\( 1<\frac{\pi}{2} \)
    so that \( \sin\left(1\right)<\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=1 \)). Therefore a common
    application of the Banach fixed point theorem for the complete metric space
    \( \left(\left[0;1\right],|\cdot|\right) \) tells us not only that there is a unique fixed point
    of \( \cos \) over \( \left[0;1\right] \) (we already know this information) but also the way
    the fixed point is constructed. Take any \( x\in\left[0;1\right] \), the fixed point is the
    limit (\( \left[0;1\right] \) is complete) of the sequence
    \( \left(\cos^{\circ n}\left(x\right)\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\left[0;1\right]^{\mathbb{N}} \)
    where \( \cos^{\circ n} \) denotes the functional composition of \( \cos \) with itself \( n \)
    times (\( \cos^{\circ 0}=Id \)). This means that \( \lim
    _{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left(\cos|_{\left[0;1\right]}\right)^{\circ n}=\mathrm{cte}_{\alpha} \)
    where \( \alpha \) is the Dottie number. Apparently the generalized case
    \( \cos\left(z\right)=z \) where \( z\in\mathbb{C} \) has infinitely many solutions (it uses
    Picard's theorem).

    \begin{lemma}
        Let \( I \subset \mathbb{R} \) be a connected set (equivalently, \( I \) is an interval,
        that is, \( \forall x,y \in I,\ \left[ x,y \right] \cup \left[ y,x \right] \subset I \)).
        Let \( h : I \to \mathbb{R} \) be an injective continuous function. Then \( h \) is strictly
        monotone.
    \end{lemma}
    \begin{proof}
        We define the set of ordered pairs from the domain \( I \) as follows:
        \[
            D = <_{I\times I}=\left\{ \left(x,y\right) \in I \times I \mid x < y \right\} \subset I
            \times I .
        \]
        Since \( I \) is an interval, the set \( D \) is convex. Indeed, let \( A =
        \left(x_1,y_1\right) \) and \( B = \left(x_2,y_2\right) \) be points in \( D \), and let
        \( \lambda \in \left[ 0,1 \right] \). Since \( x_1 < y_1 \), \( x_2 < y_2 \), and
        \( \lambda, 1-\lambda \geq 0 \), the convex combination satisfies
        \[
            \lambda x_1 + \left(1-\lambda\right)x_2 < \lambda y_1 + \left(1-\lambda\right)y_2 .
        \]
        Moreover, since \( I \) is an interval, equivalently a convex set, we have
        \[
            \lambda x_1 + \left(1-\lambda\right)x_2 \in I \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda y_1 +
            \left(1-\lambda\right)y_2 \in I .
        \]
        Thus, \( \lambda A + \left(1-\lambda\right)B \in D \), and hence \( D \) is convex. In
        particular, \( D \) is path-connected and therefore connected.
        \\\\
        We now define a difference function \( g : D \to \mathbb{R} \) by
        \[
            g\left(x,y\right) = h\left(y\right) - h\left(x\right).
        \]
        Since \( h \) is continuous on \( I \), the function \( g \) is continuous on \( D \), being
        the composition
        \[
            D \xhookrightarrow{\ \iota\ } I \times I \xrightarrow{\, h \times h \,} \mathbb{R}
            \times \mathbb{R} \xrightarrow{\ -\ } \mathbb{R}.
        \]
        Moreover, since \( h \) is injective, the function \( g \) never vanishes, that is,
        \( 0 \notin g\left[ D \right] \). Recall that the image of a connected set under a
        continuous map is connected; therefore, \( g\left[ D \right] \) is a connected subset of
        \( \mathbb{R} \), hence an interval. Consequently,
        \[
            g\left[ D \right] \subset \mathbb{R}_{>0},
        \]
        in which case \( h \) is strictly increasing, or
        \[
            g\left[ D \right] \subset \mathbb{R}_{<0},
        \]
        in which case \( h \) is strictly decreasing. This concludes the proof.
    \end{proof}


    -Now we can prove the result: we argue by contradiction. Let us fix a real continuous function
    \( f:\left(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)\longrightarrow\left(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{R}}\right) \)
    with the property that \( f\circ f=\cos \). Then we know by our second result that
    \( f\left(\alpha\right)=\alpha \) and \( f|_{\left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right]} \) is an injection.
    Since \( f \) is continuous, \( f|_{\left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right]} \) must be a continuous
    injection. By our third result \( f|_{\left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right]} \) is strictly monotone.
    Since \( f\left(\alpha\right)=\alpha\in]0;1[\subset \left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right] \), and
    \( f|_{\left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right]} \) is continuous we have that \( \alpha\in
    \left(f|_{\left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right]}\right)^{-1}\big[]0;1[\big]=:U\in\mathcal{T}_{\left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right]}^{\mathbb{R}} \).
    In this case, the composition \( f\circ f \) must be strictly increasing over \( U \); for if
    \( x,y\in U\subset \left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right] \) with \( x<y \) then by construction of
    \( U \) we have \( f\left(x\right),f\left(y\right)\in \left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right] \)
    (important). Now by the strict monotonicity of \( f|_{\left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right]} \), we have
    two cases. If \( f|_{\left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right]} \) is strictly increasing then
    \( f\left(x\right)<f\left(y\right) \) so that again we have
    \( f\left(f\left(x\right)\right)<f\left(f\left(y\right)\right) \). If
    \( f|_{\left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right]} \) is strictly decreasing then
    \( f\left(x\right)>f\left(y\right) \) and so
    \( f\left(f\left(x\right)\right)<f\left(f\left(y\right)\right) \). In all case \( \left(f\circ
    f\right)\left(x\right)<\left(f\circ f\right)\left(y\right) \). Therefore \( \cos|_U=\left(f\circ
    f\right)|_U \) is strictly increasing. This is a contradiction with the fact that \( \cos \) is
    strictly decreasing over \( \left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\supset U \) \textbf{if} \( U \)
    contains at least \( 2 \) elements. The latter is true: by construction \( U \) is of the form
    \( \left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\cap V \) with \( V \) an open set of \( \mathbb{R} \), in
    particular (and by construction of \( \mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{R}} \)) \( V \) contains a basis
    element (which is an open interval \( ]c,d[ \) of positive length) containing \( \alpha \).
    Therefore:
    \[
        \alpha\in
        ]c,d\left[\cap\right]0;1\left[\subset\right]c,d[\cap\left[0;\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\subset U
    \]
    so \( \varnothing\subsetneq[\alpha;\min\left\{d,1\right\}[\subset U \), and \( U \) necessarily
    contains infinitely many points.

    \begin{lemma} Let \( f: \mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R} \) be a continuous function. Suppose there
        exists a connected open set \( U \subset \mathbb{R} \) such that \( \left.f \right|_{U} \)
        is strictly decreasing. If \( f \) has a unique fixed point \( \alpha\in\mathbb{R} \) which
        in addition satisfies \( \alpha \in U \), then there is no continuous function \( g:
        \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} \) such that \( f = g \circ g \).
    \end{lemma}
    \begin{proof}
        Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a continuous function \( g: \mathbb{R} \to
        \mathbb{R} \) such that \( g \circ g = f \). Consider the fixed point \( \alpha \in U \). We
        have
        \[
            g\left(\alpha\right) = g\bigl( f\left(\alpha\right) \bigr) = g\bigl( g\bigl(
                g\left(\alpha\right) \bigr) \bigr) = f\bigl( g\left(\alpha\right) \bigr).
        \]
        Thus \( g\left(\alpha\right) \) is a fixed point of \( f \), and by its uniqueness we obtain
        \( g\left(\alpha\right) = \alpha \in U \). In particular, by continuity of \( g \), there
        exists an open interval \( I \) with \( \alpha \in I \subset U \) such that
        \( g\left[I\right] \subset U \).
        \\\\
        Now observe that since \( \left. f \right|_{U} = \left. \left( g \circ g \right)
        \right|_{U}=g \circ \left(\left. g \right|_{U}\right) \) is strictly decreasing, it is
        injective. Thus \( \left. g \right|_{U} \) is injective. Since \( g \) is continuous and
        injective on the connected set \( U \), \( \left. g \right|_{U} \) must be strictly
        monotone, and hence \( \left. g \right|_{I} \) is also strictly monotone.
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item If \( \left. g \right|_{I} \) is strictly increasing, then \( \left. g
                \right|_{U} \) is strictly increasing (as \( I\subset U \)), and since
                \( g\left[I\right] \subset U \) we have that \( \left. \left( g \circ g \right)
                \right|_{I} \) is strictly increasing.
            \item If \( \left. g \right|_{I} \) is strictly decreasing, then \( \left. g
                \right|_{U} \) is strictly decreasing (as \( I\subset U \)), and since
                \( g\left[I\right] \subset U \) we have that \( \left. \left( g \circ g \right)
                \right|_{I} \) is strictly increasing.
        \end{enumerate}
        In all cases, \( \left. f \right|_{I} = \left. \left( g \circ g \right) \right|_{I} \) must
        be strictly increasing. This contradicts the initial assumption that \( \left. f
        \right|_{U} \) is strictly decreasing, and hence that \( \left. f \right|_{I} \) is strictly
        decreasing: indeed, \( \alpha \in I \) and \( I \) is an interval, hence \( I \) contains at
        least \( 2 \) elements (in fact uncountably many), which provide the intended
        contradiction\footnote{If \( S \subseteq \mathbb{R} \) and \( h : S \to \mathbb{R} \) is a
        function then: \( h \) is both strictly increasing and strictly decreasing if and only if
        \( \left\lvert S \right\rvert \leq 1 \)}. Therefore, no such continuous \( g \) exists.
    \end{proof}

    \begin{lemma}
        Let \( f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} \) be a strictly increasing continuous function. Suppose
        that on any interval \( \left(a, b\right) \) where \( a \) and \( b \) are adjacent fixed
        points (i.e., \( f\left(a\right)=a, f\left(b\right)=b \) and \( f\left(x\right) \neq x \)
        for \( x \in \left(a, b\right) \)), the function satisfies either \( f\left(x\right) > x \)
        or \( f\left(x\right) < x \). Then there exists a continuous strictly increasing function
        \( g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} \) such that \( g \circ g = f \).
    \end{lemma}

    \begin{proof}
        Since \( f \) is strictly increasing we could consider \( \pm \infty \) as fixed point. So
        there is always fixed point, We provide the construction for an interval \( \left(a,
        b\right) \) of fixed point \( a<b \) where \( f\left(x\right) > x \). The case for
        \( f\left(x\right) < x \) is analogous, and the global function is formed by the union of
        such constructions and the identity on the set of fixed points.

        \begin{enumerate}
            \item \textit{Fundamental Interval:} Pick an arbitrary point \( x_0 \in \left(a,
                b\right) \). Let \( x_1 = f\left(x_0\right) \). Since \( f\left(x\right) > x \), we
                have \( x_0 < x_1 \). We define the sequence \( x_{n+1} = f\left(x_n\right) \) for
                all \( n \in \mathbb{Z} \).

            \item \textit{Seed Map:} Choose a value \( c \) such that \( x_0 < c < x_1 \). Define
                \( g \) on the interval \( \left[x_0, c\right] \) as any strictly increasing
                continuous function such that \( g\left(x_0\right) = c \) and \( g\left(c\right) =
                x_1 \).

            \item \textit{Functional Extension:} For \( x \in \left[c, x_1\right] \), we are forced
                by the requirement \( g\left(g\left(x\right)\right) = f\left(x\right) \) to define:
                \[
                    g\left(x\right) = f\left(g^{-1}\left(x\right)\right)
                \]
                Because \( g: \left[x_0, c\right] \to \left[c, x_1\right] \) is a bijection,
                \( g^{-1} \) is well-defined. This defines \( g \) on the entire "fundamental
                interval" \( \left[x_0, x_1\right] \).

            \item \textit{Inductive Propagation:} We extend \( g \) to the rest of \( \left(a,
                b\right) \) using the identity:
                \[
                    g\left(x\right) = f\left(g\left(f^{-1}\left(x\right)\right)\right)
                \]
                If \( g \) is defined on \( \left[x_n, x_{n+1}\right] \), this relation defines it
                on \( \left[x_{n+1}, x_{n+2}\right] \). Since \( f \) and \( f^{-1} \) are
                continuous and strictly increasing, \( g \) remains continuous and strictly
                increasing across the nodes \( x_n \).

            \item \textit{Boundary Conditions:} Set \( g\left(a\right) = a \) and \( g\left(b\right)
                = b \). Since \( \lim_{n \to -\infty} x_n = a \) and \( \lim_{n \to \infty} x_n =
                b \), the monotonic nature of the construction ensures continuity at the fixed
                points.
        \end{enumerate}
        Thus, \( g \) is a continuous iterative square root of \( f \).
    \end{proof}
    \begin{lemma}
        If \( f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} \) is continuous and there exist \( n > 0 \) and
        \( a \in \mathbb{R} \) such that \( f^{\circ n}\left(a\right) = a \), then \( f \) admits a
        fixed point.
    \end{lemma}

    \begin{proof}
        Consider the function \( g\left(x\right) = f\left(x\right) - x \), which is continuous.
        Assume by contradiction that \( f \) has no fixed point. Then \( g\left(x\right) \) never
        vanishes, and by the Intermediate Value Theorem, it must maintain a constant sign on
        \( \mathbb{R} \).

        Without loss of generality, assume \( g\left(x\right) > 0 \) for all \( x \in \mathbb{R} \).
        This implies that \( f\left(x\right) > x \) for all \( x \). Applying this inequality
        inductively to the iterates of \( a \), we get:
        \[
            f\left(a\right) > a
        \]
        \[
            f^{\circ 2}\left(a\right) > f\left(a\right)
        \]
        \[
            \vdots
        \]
        \[
            f^{\circ n}\left(a\right) > f^{\circ n-1}\left(a\right)
        \]

        Combining these inequalities yields:
        \[
            a = f^{\circ n}\left(a\right) > f^{\circ n-1}\left(a\right) > \dots > f\left(a\right) >
            a
        \]
        This results in \( a > a \), which is a contradiction. Thus, \( f \) must admit a fixed
        point.
    \end{proof}
