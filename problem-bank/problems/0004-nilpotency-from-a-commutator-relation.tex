\begin{problem}
    Let \( \alpha \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{0\right\} \) and suppose that \( F \) and \( G \)
    are linear maps (operators) from \( \mathbb{R}^n \) into \( \mathbb{R}^n \) satisfying
    \[
        F \circ G - G \circ F = \alpha F.
    \]

    (a) Show that for all \( k \in \mathbb{N} \) one has
    \[
        F^k \circ G - G \circ F^k = \alpha k F^k.
    \]

    (b) Show that there exists \( k \geq 1 \) such that \( F^k = 0 \).
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
    \textit{Solution 1.}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item[(a)] We prove the result by induction on \( k \). The base case \( k = 1 \) is given
            by the assumption. Assume that the formula holds for some \( k \geq 1 \), i.e.,
            \[
                F^k \circ G - G \circ F^k = \alpha k F^k.
            \]
            We compute:
            \begin{align*}
                F^{k+1} \circ G - G \circ F^{k+1}
                &= F^k \circ(F \circ G) - G \circ F^k \circ F \\
                &= F^k \circ(G \circ F + \alpha F) - G \circ F^k\circ F \\
                &= F^k\circ G \circ F + \alpha F^{k+1} - G \circ F^{k}\circ F \\
                &= (F^k \circ G - G\circ F^k) \circ F + \alpha F^{k+1} \\
                &= (\alpha k F^k) \circ F + \alpha F^{k+1} \\
                &= \alpha k F^{k+1} + \alpha F^{k+1} \\
                &= \alpha (k+1) F^{k+1}.
            \end{align*}
            This completes the induction step, proving the result.

        \item[(b)] Consider the linear operator \( L \) defined on the space of \( n \times n \)
            matrices by
            \[
                L\left(F\right) = F \circ G - G \circ F.
            \]
            By the given assumption, we have \( L\left(F\right) = \alpha F \). Also using part (a),
            we obtain
            \[
                L\left(F^k\right) = \alpha k F^k.
            \]
            The operator \( L \) acts on an \( n^2 \)-dimensional space and can have at most
            \( n^2 \) distinct eigenvalues. However, if \( F^k \neq 0 \) for all \( k \), then
            \( L \) has infinitely many eigenvalues \( \alpha k \), which contradicts the fact that
            the space is finite-dimensional. Hence, there must exist some \( k \geq 1 \) such that
            \( F^k = 0 \).
    \end{enumerate}

    \textit{Solution 2.}
    Part (a) strongly hints at induction. So let's try it. The base case \( k = 1 \) is precisely
    our condition
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:p13-condition} AB - BA = \alpha A \quad .
    \end{equation}

    ~\\
    So let's assume the equality holds for \( k \), and we wish to show it for \( k+1 \). We have:
    \begin{align*}
        A^{k+1}B & \underset{\clap{\tiny \text{condition \eqref{eq:p13-condition}}}}{=} A^k \cdot (\alpha A + BA)        \\
        & \underset{\clap{\tiny \text{inductive hypothesis}}}{=} \alpha A^{k+1} + (k \alpha A^k + BA^k) \cdot A \\
        & \overset{\checkmark}{=} (k + 1) \alpha A^{k+1} + BA^{k+1} \quad .
    \end{align*}
    This completes the induction step and hence the proof of part (a).

    ~\\ \\
    Part (b) becomes a one-liner using Jacobson's Lemma: Let \( C \coloneqq AB - BA \) be the
    commutator of \( A \) and \( B \). Then \( A \) and \( C \) commute:
    \[ AC = \alpha A^2 = CA \quad . \]
    Using Jacobson's Lemma, we have that \( C \) is nilpotent. And since \( A = \frac{1}{\alpha}
    C \), it follows that \( A \) itself is nilpotent, proving that there is \( m \in
    \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1} \) such that \( A^m = 0 \).

    ~\\
    Alternatively, observe that the left-hand side \( A^kB - BA^k \) in part (a) depends linearly on
    \( A^k \). This motivates the linear map
    \[ f: \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} \to \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}, \ X \mapsto XB - BX \quad , \]
    where \( A \) and \( B \) are \( n \times n \) matrices. Then, the result from part (a) states
    that
    \[ f(A^k) = k \alpha A^k \text{ for all } k \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1} \quad . \]
    Now, assume for the sake of contradiction that \( A^m \neq \mathbf{0} \) for all \( m \in
    \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1} \). Thus, \( A^m \neq \mathbf{0} \) is an eigenvector of \( f \) to the
    eigenvalue \( m \alpha \) for all \( m \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1} \). And since \( \alpha \neq
    0 \), we see that \( f \) has an infinite number of eigenvalues. This, of course, is a
    contradiction, since \( f \) is an endomorphism of the finite-dimensional
    \( \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} \).
\end{solution}
